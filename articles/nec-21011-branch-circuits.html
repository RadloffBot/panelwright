The rule in one sentence

In a dwelling unit, the total calculated load (Article 220) sets how many amps the wiring must carry — but 210.11(C) is a count rule: regardless of load, the unit must also have two or more 20 A small-appliance circuits, at least one 20 A laundry circuit, one or more 120 V 20 A bathroom circuits, and (if there is a garage with electric power) at least one 120 V 20 A garage circuit. Each of those required circuits also puts 1,500 VA on the feeder (220.52) — so the minimum-circuit rule and the load calculation are tied together.

The code text (verbatim)

210.11 Branch Circuits Required

“Branch circuits for lighting and for appliances, including motor-operated appliances, shall be provided to supply the loads calculated in accordance with 220.10. In addition, branch circuits shall be provided for specific loads not covered by 220.10 where required elsewhere in this Code and for dwelling unit loads as specified in 210.11(C).” Source: NEC 2020 full-code text (NFPA 70), verbatim. (The 2020 scan’s OCR mangles “this Code” as “this Colle” — corrected here; the 2023 edition text, per the Mike Holt 2023-NEC Article 210 summary, reads “this Code.”)

(A) Number of Branch Circuits

“The minimum number of branch circuits shall be determined from the total calculated load and the size or rating of the circuits used. In all installations, the number of circuits shall be sufficient to supply the load served. In no case shall the load on any circuit exceed the maximum specified by 220.18.” Source: NEC 2020 full-code text, verbatim (OCR “installations” normalized from the scan’s “installa tions”).

(B) Load Evenly Proportioned Among Branch Circuits

“Where the load is calculated on the basis of volt-amperes per square meter or per square foot, the wiring system up to and including the branch-circuit panelboard(s) shall be provided to serve not less than the calculated load. This load shall be evenly proportioned among multioutlet branch circuits within the panelboard(s). Branch-circuit overcurrent devices and circuits shall be required to be installed only to serve the connected load.” Source: NEC 2020 full-code text, verbatim (OCR “Where” normalized from the scan’s “Vihere”). Note: (B) is a load-distribution rule — it is NOT a “two lighting circuits” requirement (see the gotchas below).

(C) Dwelling Units

“(1) Small-Appliance Branch Circuits. In addition to the number of branch circuits required by other parts of this section, two or more 20-ampere small-appliance branch circuits shall be provided for all receptacle outlets specified by 210.52(B). (2) Laundry Branch Circuits. In addition to the number of branch circuits required by other parts of this section, at least one additional 20-ampere branch circuit shall be provided to supply the laundry receptacle outlet(s) required by 210.52(F). This circuit shall have no other outlets. (3) Bathroom Branch Circuits. In addition to the number of branch circuits required by other parts of this section, one or more 120-volt, 20-ampere branch circuit shall be provided to supply bathroom(s) receptacle outlet(s) required by 210.52(D) and any countertop and similar work surface receptacle outlets. Such circuits shall have no other outlets. Exception: Where the 20-ampere circuit supplies a single bathroom, outlets for other equipment within the same bathroom shall be permitted to be supplied in accordance with 210.23(A)(1) and (A)(2). (4) Garage Branch Circuits. In addition to the number of branch circuits required by other parts of this section, at least one 120-volt, 20-ampere branch circuit shall be installed to supply receptacle outlets required by 210.52(G)(1) for attached garages and in detached garages with electric power. This circuit shall have no other outlets. Exception: This circuit shall be permitted to supply readily accessible outdoor receptacle outlets.” Source: NEC 2020 full-code text (NFPA 70), verbatim, with OCR scan artifacts normalized: the scan prints “installa‐tions” (hyphenated) as “installations”, “Vihere” as “Where”, “shall have no otl1er outlets” as “shall have no other outlets”, “210.52(G)(])” as “210.52(G)(1)”, “210.23(A)(l)” as “210.23(A)(1)”, and “permitted lo be supplied” as “permitted to be supplied”. Each normalization is an obvious OCR repair (digit/letter swaps and a stray hyphen), and the corrected wording is confirmed by the 2023 edition text (Mike Holt 2023-NEC Article 210 summary, which restates (C)(1)–(C)(4)). Note: the 2014 extract on disk is Article 220 only and does not reproduce the 210.11 body — it does, however, print the cross-references “as covered by 210.11(C)(1)” (220.52) and “210.11(C)(1) and (C)(2)” (220.82), confirming the (C)(1)/(C)(2) citation structure predates 2020. Confirmed unchanged in the 2023 NEC (Mike Holt 2023-NEC Article 210 summary: (C)(1) two 20A small-appliance per 210.52(B); (C)(2) one 20A laundry per 210.52(F), “can supply other receptacles in the laundry area, but not lighting outlets or receptacle outlets outside the laundry area”; (C)(3) one or more 20A bathroom per 210.52(D), “cannot supply lighting outlets or any other receptacle outlets”, single-bathroom exception per 210.23(A)(1)/(A)(2); (C)(4) one 20A garage per 210.52(G)(1), two exceptions).

Three structural things to notice. First, (C)(1)–(C)(4) are additive: “in addition to the number of branch circuits required by other parts of this section” — they sit on top of the load-based count from (A), not in place of it. Second, (C) has no outdoor item and no (C)(5): outdoor receptacles are covered only insofar as the garage circuit’s exception permits them. Third, the cross-references do the real work — each circuit type exists to serve the receptacle outlets that 210.52(B)/(D)/(F)/(G)(1) require. Get the outlets right and the circuits follow.

Only the small-appliance and laundry circuits carry a 220.52 load line — 1,500 VA per circuit — because 220.52(A)/(B) names exactly those two circuit types. Bathroom and garage circuits are still on the panel and still count against the space you have, but they add no separate 220.52 VA; their receptacle load rides with the general lighting and receptacle calculation of 220.14.

The two gotchas (and one that isn’t a rule)

Gotcha 1 — the bathroom circuit is the most exclusive

210.11(C)(3) is stricter than it looks. The circuit serves the 210.52(D) bathroom receptacle(s) and countertop/work-surface receptacle outlets, and “shall have no other outlets.” The only escape valve is the exception: if that one 20 A circuit supplies a single bathroom, outlets for other equipment within the same bathroom (a fan, a light, a towel-warmer outlet) are permitted per 210.23(A)(1) and (A)(2). The moment a second bathroom is on the same circuit, the exception is gone — no other-bathroom equipment outlets, no lighting, no hallway receptacle. This is the requirement the PanelWright checklist flags first.

Gotcha 2 — the garage circuit is the only one with an “outdoor” allowance

210.11(C)(4)’s exception is the sole place in 210.11(C) that lets a required circuit leave its zone: the garage circuit may supply readily accessible outdoor receptacle outlets. So a dedicated outdoor circuit is common good practice (and GFCI-protected per 210.8), but it is not a 210.11 requirement — there is no (C)(5) for outdoor receptacles, and the code does not mandate a separate outdoor circuit at all.

The “two lighting circuits” myth

210.11 has no lighting-circuit minimum. People often say “210.11 requires two lighting circuits” — it does not. What (B) actually requires is that a load calculated on a VA-per-square-foot basis be evenly proportioned among the multioutlet branch circuits in the panelboard — a distribution rule, not a count rule. “Two or more lighting circuits” is design practice (even distribution, serviceability), and PanelWright’s checklist lists it as a design-practice row, explicitly labeled as not a code cite.

How 210.11 feeds the load calc (220.52 and 220.82)

  • 220.52(A) small-appliance circuit load: “the load shall be calculated at 1500 volt-amperes for each 2-wire small-appliance branch circuit as covered by 210.11(C)(1).” Every required (and any additional) 2-wire small-appliance circuit is 1,500 VA on the feeder. Two minimum circuits = 3,000 VA; a permitted third = 4,500 VA (core-verified below).
  • 220.52(B) laundry circuit load: “a load of not less than 1500 volt-amperes shall be included for each 2-wire laundry branch circuit installed as covered by 210.11(C)(2).” One minimum = 1,500 VA; a second laundry circuit = 3,000 VA.
  • 220.82(B)(2) optional method: the same 1,500 VA applies per circuit in the single-dwelling optional method — “1500 volt-amperes for each 2-wire, 20-ampere small-appliance branch circuit and each laundry branch circuit covered in 210.11(C)(1) and (C)(2).” So a home with the two minimum small-appliance circuits plus one laundry circuit starts at 4,500 VA of (B)(2) load before a square foot of lighting is counted. (The 220.82 card on the main page has the small-appliance and laundry circuit-count inputs wired to exactly this.)
  • 220.42 demand still applies: 220.52(A)/(B) both say these loads “shall be permitted to be included with the general lighting load and subjected to the demand factors provided in Table 220.42” — so the 1,500 VA lines ride into the standard-method demand, they are not added on top at 100% (covered in the 220.42 lighting-demand explainer).

Where 210.11 fits (and doesn’t)

  • Dwelling units only: (C)(1)–(C)(4) are titled “Dwelling Units.” Non-dwelling occupancies get the load-based minimum from 210.11(A) and the even-proportioning rule from (B) — plus whatever specific-circuit rules apply elsewhere in the NEC.
  • Circuits vs. loads: 210.11 is the “how many dedicated circuits” side; Article 220 is the “how many amps” side. The 1,500 VA lines are where they meet (220.52).
  • Conductor and OCPD sizing is elsewhere: 210.11 tells you the circuits must exist; 210.19/210.20 size their conductors and protection (125% of continuous load), and 210.21/210.23 set receptacle ratings and permissible loads on multioutlet circuits. The bathroom exception explicitly points at 210.23(A)(1)/(A)(2).
  • Receptacles are 210.52: each of the four circuit types exists to serve a 210.52 outlet set — (B) small-appliance rooms, (D) bathrooms, (F) laundry, (G)(1) garages. Counting required circuits without counting the required receptacles is how these get missed.

Editions: 2014 / 2017 / 2020 / 2023 / 2026

  • 2014: the four (C)(1)–(C)(4) minimums in the form above — the NFPA 70 2014 extract on disk cites 210.11(C)(1)/(C)(2)/(C)(3) from the 2014 text (e.g., the 220.44 cross-reference list) and its 220.18 text is consistent with the 2020 structure. This article quotes the 2020 full-code text as the verbatim anchor because it is the cleanest edition on hand.
  • 2017 / 2020: 210.11 is unchanged in structure — the same four (C) items with the same exceptions. The 2020 scan’s text was read verbatim this session (OCR artifacts normalized and listed in the code block above).
  • 2023: no change. The 2023 NEC change analysis on disk makes no 210.11 entry, and the Mike Holt 2023-NEC “Branch Circuits” summary (fetched this session) restates (C)(1)–(C)(4) exactly as above — including the single-bathroom exception and the garage outdoor-receptacle exception.
  • 2026: the NEC renumbers load calculations out of Article 220 into the Article 120 area (flagged in the tool’s UI, as with the 220.42/220.54/220.56 articles); 210.11-specific 2026 changes are not independently verified here — verify against the adopted edition.
